What Is a Normal BMI? Comparing Chinese, WHO and Asia-Pacific Standards

The same height and weight can produce opposite conclusions depending on which guideline you open. Someone classified as obese under the Chinese standard can be merely overweight under the WHO standard; someone perfectly normal in China can be overweight under the Asia-Pacific cutoffs. The BMI number itself is arithmetic; the category is a human decision. This guide puts the three most widely cited standards side by side, gives weight ranges you can look up by height, and walks through three real examples.

1. Three Standards Side by Side

All three standards agree on one thing: the underweight line sits at 18.5. Every disagreement is about where "normal" ends and "overweight" begins.

The chart shows each standard's character clearly. WHO international is the most permissive, with the longest healthy band. Asia-Pacific is the strictest, calling 23 overweight. China sits in between but is noticeably tighter than WHO. The reason for the stricter Asian thresholds is that at the same BMI, Asian populations tend to carry more visceral fat and face higher metabolic risk than European and North American populations.

Two further variants are worth knowing. The Japan Society for the Study of Obesity treats 25 as the start of obesity rather than overweight and grades it from 1 to 4 (25 to 29.9, 30 to 34.9, 35 to 39.9, and 40 or above), meaning there is no "overweight" band at all in that system. Some Southeast Asian clinical settings set the overweight line at 23 as well. So the honest answer to "which category am I in" is always "under which standard".

The awkward rows are 24 and 25. At 24, China already says overweight while WHO still says normal. At 25, Asia-Pacific has moved to obese while China still says overweight. In other words, the same health report can shift a full category depending on the guideline printed next to it. That is why Chinese hospitals and health check centres use the national standard while international research papers usually cite WHO cutoffs. When you read your own result, check which standard the report used before you worry about the number.

4. Three Worked Examples

Example 1: 175 cm, 70 kg. BMI is 70 divided by 1.75 squared, about 22.9. All three standards call this normal, so it is the least controversial zone.

Example 2: 170 cm, 85 kg. BMI is about 29.4. China classifies this as obese (the threshold is 28), WHO still calls it overweight (obesity starts at 30), and Asia-Pacific agrees with China. This is the classic one-category gap between Chinese and WHO cutoffs.

Example 3: 180 cm, 75 kg. BMI is about 23.1. China says normal, WHO says normal, but Asia-Pacific already says overweight. One person, one pair of measurements, two different verdicts.

All three examples point to the same conclusion: BMI produces a number, but whether that number counts as normal depends on the standard you apply. If you lift weights, your weight can rise while your body fat falls, and BMI becomes a poor guide. In that case track your waist measurement and body fat percentage alongside it.

5. Three Limits of BMI

  • It cannot separate muscle from fat. Muscle is denser than fat, so a fit person with 15% body fat can still be labelled overweight by BMI. Many athletes carry a permanently high BMI without carrying excess risk.
  • It cannot see where fat sits. BMI measures quantity, not distribution. Two people with a BMI of 24 can have very different metabolic risk if one stores fat around the abdomen. This is the basis of so-called normal-weight obesity.
  • It does not apply to everyone. Pregnant women, children and teenagers, adults over 65, and people with very low muscle mass all fall outside BMI's useful range. Children need age- and sex-specific growth curves, and older adults need separate attention for muscle loss.

In short, BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnosis. It buys extremely low measurement cost with two numbers, and the price is precision.

6. What to Do with Your Number

If your BMI runs high, adjust the structure of your diet rather than simply eating less. Cut sugary drinks, refined carbohydrates and deep-fried food, keep protein intake steady, and combine aerobic exercise with resistance training. Losing 0.5 to 1 kg per week is a sustainable pace that preserves muscle.

If your BMI runs low, first rule out thyroid and digestive conditions with a clinician. Then raise intake of quality protein (eggs, dairy, fish, soy) and complex carbohydrates (whole grains, potatoes) while adding resistance training, so the gain is muscle rather than fat.

One expectation to set: BMI moves far more slowly than it feels. Going from 28 to 24 at 170 cm means losing roughly 11 kg, which normally takes months rather than weeks. If you need to manage intake, use the calorie calculator to estimate daily expenditure and break the target into small steps.

8. Three Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: a normal BMI means healthy. Plenty of people sit inside the normal band with a waist that exceeds the recommended limit. Measure your waist as well; above 90 cm for men or 85 cm for women signals central obesity even when BMI looks fine.

Misconception 2: one standard applies to everyone. Chinese, WHO and Asia-Pacific guidelines can place the same person a full category apart. When you compare your report against someone else's, confirm both used the same standard, or the comparison means nothing.

Misconception 3: a single reading tells the story. Hydration, recent meals and digestion can move the scale by a kilo or two. Tracking at a fixed time and watching the trend over three months is far more informative. You can record your age alongside it using the age calculator for a longitudinal view.

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Which BMI standard should I use?

Use the standard your clinician or health check report uses. In mainland China that is usually WS/T 428-2013. If you are comparing against international research, WHO cutoffs are the common reference.

Is BMI still useful if it has so many limits?

Yes. It is cheap, reproducible and good enough for population screening. It simply should not be the only number you track, and it should never be treated as a diagnosis.

Why is the Asian threshold stricter?

At the same BMI, Asian populations tend to accumulate more visceral fat and develop metabolic complications at lower BMI values, so the cutoffs were lowered to flag risk earlier.

Can BMI be used for children?

Not with adult categories. Children and teenagers are assessed against age- and sex-specific percentile charts, because body composition changes naturally through growth.

Does BMI work for athletes and bodybuilders?

Poorly. High muscle mass pushes BMI up without excess fat, so athletes should rely on body fat percentage and waist measurement instead.

10. Practical Tips

  • Weigh at the same time each day: morning, fasted and after using the bathroom gives the most stable reading.
  • Always pair BMI with waist measurement: the combination catches metabolic risk that BMI alone misses, especially after 40.
  • Ignore BMI if you train seriously: for regular lifters, body fat percentage is the better number.
  • Lose weight at 0.5 to 1 kg per week: faster loss tends to strip muscle and rebound.
  • Confirm the standard before reacting: a full category can hinge on which guideline is being used.
