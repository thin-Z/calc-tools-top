BMI Calculator Guide

BMI (Body Mass Index) is a simple screening number that estimates whether your weight is in a healthy range for your height. The formula is weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared: BMI = kg ÷ m². It is used worldwide for adults roughly aged 18 to 65, with well-known exceptions covered later (pregnant women, children, and very muscular athletes). You enter your height and weight, the calculator does the division locally in your browser, and nothing you type is ever uploaded to a server.

Two Classification Standards You Should Know

One number can land in different categories depending on which standard you apply. The WHO international standard is the most widely cited, but for adults of Asian descent many health authorities — including China's WS/T 428—2013 — use slightly stricter cut-offs, because the same BMI tends to carry more metabolic risk in Asian populations. Three Worked Examples

Walk through the formula yourself to double-check the calculator. Remember: BMI = weight ÷ (height in meters)².

  1. 175 cm, 70 kg: 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.86 → Normal (comfortably inside the healthy band on both standards).
  2. 170 cm, 75 kg: 75 ÷ (1.70 × 1.70) = 75 ÷ 2.89 = 25.95 → Overweight (24.0–27.9 on the Chinese chart, and also 25–29.9 on WHO).
  3. 180 cm, 95 kg: 95 ÷ (1.80 × 1.80) = 95 ÷ 3.24 = 29.32 → Obese (≥ 28.0 in China; WHO would still call this overweight, just under 30).

Notice the same 95 kg is "obese" in China but only "overweight" by WHO — a good reminder that the standard matters. BMI is only a starting point: it does not tell muscle from fat, nor where fat is stored. BMI Calculator FAQ

Which standard should I use, China or WHO?

For adults in mainland China, prefer the WS/T 428—2013 thresholds: normal 18.5–23.9, overweight 24.0–27.9, obese ≥ 28.0. They are stricter than the WHO international cut-offs (normal up to 24.9, obese from 30.0) because the same BMI carries more risk in Asian bodies. This tool reports the Chinese category by default so you are not misled by a single threshold.

Does a normal BMI mean I am healthy?

Not necessarily. BMI only compares weight to height; it says nothing about fat distribution. Even with a normal BMI, if your waistline is large (China's cut-offs: men ≥ 90 cm, women ≥ 85 cm) you are still in the "central obesity" risk group with higher cardiovascular and metabolic risk. A normal BMI plus a normal waist is the safer combination.

Why do fit, muscular people show a high BMI?

Muscle is denser than fat, so a strength-trained athlete can weigh more for the same height and post an "overweight" or even "obese" BMI while carrying very little fat. For these people, body-fat percentage (roughly above 25% for men, 30% for women) and girth measurements are far more meaningful than BMI.

Can pregnant women and children use this BMI?

Not directly. Pregnant women should use their pre-pregnancy BMI to guide healthy weight gain, since adult thresholds do not apply during pregnancy. Children and teens must use age- and sex-specific BMI percentiles (such as China's 7–18 screening standard or the WHO growth standard); applying the adult 18.5 / 24 / 28 lines would give wrong answers.

How do I measure waist circumference correctly?

Stand naturally and exhale gently; wrap a tape horizontally about 1 cm above the navel (or at the midpoint between the lower rib and the hip bone). Do not suck in your stomach and do not pull the tape tight; read to 0.1 cm. Measure at the same time of day and in the same posture each time so readings are comparable.

Why does my BMI seem to change during the day?

Body weight can swing 0.5–1 kg in a day from food, drink, and sweat, which translates to roughly 0.2–0.4 BMI points. Weigh yourself first thing in the morning, after using the bathroom and before eating, and watch the long-term trend rather than any single reading. If you want to express a change as a share of your starting weight, the percentage calculator does that quickly.

Three Common BMI Myths

Myth 1: Same BMI means same health risk

Two people can both have a BMI of 24 — one sedentary with a 95 cm waist, the other training regularly with a 78 cm waist — yet face very different metabolic risk. BMI is just weight ÷ height²; it cannot see where fat sits or how much muscle you carry. Always read BMI together with waist size, body fat, and activity level.

Myth 2: Getting BMI "back to normal" solves everything

Losing weight is not the same as getting healthier. Crash diets shed mostly water and muscle, rebound easily, and can lower your resting metabolism. A better goal is slow loss (about 0.5–1 kg per week), keeping muscle, and improving waist size and fitness — not just the number on the scale.

Myth 3: A high BMI means you must diet immediately

Someone just over the normal line (say BMI 24–27, "overweight") with no other risk factors may not need strict dieting. Compared with harsh restriction, more daily activity and smarter eating (less sugary drink, more protein and vegetables) is usually more sustainable. If high blood pressure, high blood sugar, or a large waist are also present, then a more active plan makes sense.
