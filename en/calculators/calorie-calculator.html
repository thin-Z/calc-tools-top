How this calculator works: BMR, TDEE and where the ranges come from

Enter your sex, age, height, weight and activity level. The tool first estimates your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, multiplies it by an activity factor to get your daily maintenance calories (TDEE), and then centres a weight-loss and a muscle-gain range on that number. Everything runs locally in your browser.

Two equations: which one is the headline number

You get two BMR figures. Mifflin-St Jeor (1990) is the headline result and the equation most nutrition practitioners use today; Harris-Benedict (1984 revision) is shown for comparison. The older equation tends to overestimate slightly for modern populations, so if the two numbers sit far apart it usually means your body composition (lean mass) does not match the equation's assumptions — trust your measured weight trend over either formula.

Weight is in kilograms, height in centimetres, age in years. For a 30-year-old man at 175 cm and 75 kg: 10×75 + 6.25×175 − 5×30 + 5 = 1698.75, so his BMR is about 1,699 kcal.

Activity factors: who each of the five levels is for

TDEE = BMR × activity factor. This is the easiest step to get wrong, and it is the most common reason a result feels off — most people overestimate their own activity by about one level.

When in doubt, pick one level lower. One level is roughly a 10-15% difference in TDEE — about 200-300 kcal a day at a 2,000 kcal baseline, or 6,000-9,000 kcal over a month.

Where the loss and gain ranges come from

The tool centres on your TDEE: the weight-loss range is TDEE−500 to TDEE−250, and the muscle-gain range is TDEE+250 to TDEE+500. One kilogram of body fat is roughly equivalent to 7,700 kcal, so a daily deficit of 250 kcal works out to about 0.25 kg per week and a 500 kcal deficit to about 0.5 kg per week.

Why stop at 500 kcal rather than push the deficit higher? Because the bigger the deficit, the higher the risk of muscle loss and metabolic adaptation. Aggressive deficits belong in a supervised programme, not in a default setting for the general public.

Three worked examples you can compare against

  1. Man, 30, 175 cm, 75 kg, training 3-5 days a week (moderate ×1.55): BMR about 1,699 kcal, TDEE about 2,633 kcal. Weight loss: eat 2,133-2,383 kcal. Muscle gain: 2,883-3,133 kcal.
  2. Woman, 28, 162 cm, 55 kg, exercising 1-3 days a week (light ×1.375): BMR about 1,262 kcal, TDEE about 1,735 kcal. The loss range comes out at 1,235-1,485 kcal — the bottom of that range sits below her BMR. In this case keep intake near the BMR (about 1,400 kcal) and widen the deficit by moving more, not by eating less.
  3. Man, 45, 178 cm, 90 kg, sedentary (×1.2): BMR about 1,793 kcal, TDEE about 2,152 kcal. Weight loss: 1,652-1,902 kcal. Because he carries more weight than the population average, his computed TDEE is a little higher than the official reference figure for his age group (table below) even though he is sedentary.

About the Calorie Calculator

This tool answers one specific question: given your body data, how many calories a day should you eat? It does not hand you a meal plan and it does not label foods as good or bad — it gives you a reference line you can actually follow: maintenance is roughly your TDEE, fat loss sits at TDEE−250 to −500, and muscle gain at TDEE+250 to +500.

It works best alongside the rest of the health tools here. Start with the BMI Calculator to see whether you are inside the 18.5-23.9 healthy band, use the Ideal Weight Calculator to set a target weight, then convert that target into daily calories here. If you are trying to conceive or already pregnant, energy needs differ substantially from a general adult, so see the Pregnancy Due Date Calculator and follow your clinician's advice.

How to use the Calorie Calculator

  1. Select your sex and enter your age, height and current weight (your actual weight, not your target).
  2. Match the activity descriptions in the table above and pick the level closest to your real routine; when unsure, round down.
  3. Click Calculate to get your BMR, TDEE and both recommended ranges.
  4. Recalculate after roughly every 5% change in body weight.

Calorie Calculator FAQ

Why am I not losing weight when I eat exactly my TDEE?

Check three things, in order. First, whether your activity factor is one notch too high — this is the most common cause. Second, whether your food log misses cooking oil, drinks and snacks: a tablespoon of oil is around 90 kcal and a sweetened milk tea is often over 300 kcal. Third, whether your observation window is simply too short. BMR and TDEE are estimates with individual error typically around ±10%, so the reliable way to calibrate is to eat your current intake for 2-3 weeks, watch the weight trend, then adjust by 100-200 kcal — not day to day.

Can I eat below my BMR to lose weight faster?

Not advisable. Sustained intake below BMR pushes the body to adapt by reducing activity energy expenditure and burning muscle, which lowers resting metabolism, makes further loss harder and makes regain more likely. The second worked example above lands in exactly this situation: its computed loss range dips below BMR, so the fix is to keep intake near BMR and widen the deficit through activity instead.

Guidance mentions a 500-1,000 kcal cut — why does this tool only suggest 250-500?

The two are aimed at different people. Large deficits of 500-1,000 kcal a day belong in supervised programmes for people with overweight or obesity, paired with staged goals. This tool serves a broader general audience, so it defaults to the more conservative 250-500 kcal band that is easier to sustain. If your BMI is 24 or above and you need structured fat loss, a clinician or dietitian can set you up on the larger deficit safely.

Do I need to recalculate after losing weight?

Yes. BMR falls as body weight falls, because weight is the dominant variable in the equation — usually tens to a hundred-plus kcal lower than before. Recalculate after every 5% drop in body weight (for example from 70 kg down to 66-67 kg) so you do not hit the classic plateau where you are still eating the old number and nothing moves.

Should I eat back the calories my workout burned?

Do not simply add your watch or treadmill figure back to your intake. For one thing, the activity factor (×1.2-×1.9) already accounts for routine training, so adding it again double-counts. For another, the body compensates: after a hard session people tend to eat a little more and move a little less. The steadier approach is to move one activity level up when your training volume changes meaningfully (say from two sessions a week to five) rather than adjusting day by day.
