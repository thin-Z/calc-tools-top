Online Image Compression: Smaller Files, Faster Loading

This online image compressor reduces a picture's file size without noticeably hurting quality, making pages load faster, consuming less bandwidth, and making uploads smoother. All compression runs locally through your browser's Canvas API, with files never leaving your device — so it's private and gives instant feedback, a key advantage over online compressors that require uploads.

It's ideal for site operators, e-commerce sellers, content creators, and anyone who wants to optimize image size and improve user experience.

How Does Compression "Slim Down" an Image?

File size depends mainly on pixel count and how it's compressed. This tool re-encodes the image via the Canvas API, adjusting sampling and how much redundant information is kept based on your quality setting. Lower quality means smaller files but more visible loss; higher quality keeps more detail but grows the size.

JPEG is lossy and chases small size; PNG is lossless with limited room. That's why when compressing a PNG at low quality, the tool automatically converts to JPEG for better results (at the cost of transparency).

How to Use It

1. Upload or drag an image (JPG / PNG / WebP / BMP, up to 50MB).
2. Drag the "quality" slider and compare the original vs. new size on the right.
3. Preview the compressed result live and confirm the quality is acceptable.
4. When satisfied, click download to save it.

Common Compression Mistakes

① Don't chase the lowest quality. Crank quality too low and you get obvious color blocks and blur that look "dirty". For photos, 70%–85% is usually a good sweet spot.
② PNG compression can drop transparency. When a PNG is converted to JPEG for smaller size, transparency turns white. If you need transparency, keep PNG or use WebP.
③ Order of resize vs. compress. Resize to your target dimensions first, then compress — usually better, because you don't pay file size for pixels you no longer need.

How Much Should You Compress?

For images shown on the web, compress as much as you can while still looking fine, to get faster loads and lower bounce. For work that will be printed or viewed close-up, keep higher quality. Test one version, then nudge quality up or down to find the right size/quality balance.
